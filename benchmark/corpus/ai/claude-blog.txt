For most of my thirties, I was an evening person who simply never got around to exercising in the evening. Every day ended the same way: dishes, a show I only half watched, and a vague promise that tomorrow I would finally go for that run. Tomorrow had a way of filling up.

The switch happened almost by accident. Last spring our neighbour's dog started barking at 5:45 every morning, and after two weeks of lying awake and resenting it, I decided I might as well be awake on purpose. I dug my old trainers out of the hall cupboard, pulled on a hoodie, and shuffled around the block in the grey half-light. It was not a pretty run. I stopped twice. But I came home with a strange, clean feeling I had not expected.

What kept me going was not discipline. It was the quiet. At that hour the streets belong to bakery vans, a few cyclists, and the man who walks his elderly beagle in a tartan coat. Nobody needs anything from me yet. My phone stays at home. For thirty minutes the only decisions I make are left or right at the next corner.

There were practical benefits too. Running first thing means it actually happens; there is no meeting that can overrun into it, no dinner invitation to compete with. I sleep better, I drink less coffee, and I have stopped negotiating with myself every evening about whether I have the energy.

I will not pretend it is always lovely. In January it is dark, wet, and miserable, and some mornings I turn back after ten minutes. That is fine. The habit survives because the bar is low: shoes on, out the door, see what happens.

If you are thinking about trying it, my only advice is to make the first week absurdly easy. Walk if you want. Go around one block. The goal is not fitness yet; it is learning that the morning can be yours. The dog, by the way, moved away in August. I still set the alarm for 5:45.
